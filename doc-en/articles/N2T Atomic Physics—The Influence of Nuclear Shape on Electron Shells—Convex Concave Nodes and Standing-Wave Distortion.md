In standard physics, the atomic nucleus is usually treated as a point charge or a uniform sphere, but when the nucleon number deviates from magic numbers, the actual shape of the nucleus deviates from spherical. In the N2T framework, the nucleus is a node cluster—stacked from the three-dimensional fan-blade structures of multiple protons and neutrons. When the nucleon count does not satisfy the closed-shell condition, the nuclear surface nodes are distributed unevenly, producing convex and concave surfaces. These convex and concave nodes connect to the shell-layer hexagonal grid through radial edges, generating superimposed phase perturbations that cause distortions in the electron shell standing-wave modes.

Starting from the distribution of nuclear surface nodes, this article demonstrates how nuclear shape affects electron shell structure and the corresponding phenomena in standard physics.

## I. The Origin of Nuclear Shape

### 1.1 Formation of Spherical Nuclei

In the N2T framework, each nucleon (proton or neutron) is itself a three-dimensional fan-blade structure. When multiple nucleons stack around the nuclear center, their fan-blade directions compensate for each other, forming a node network.

When the nucleon count reaches a magic number (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126), the nuclear surface nodes are uniformly distributed in all directions, and the nucleus appears spherical. At this point, the nuclear surface node density is uniform, and the perturbations to the shell-layer standing-wave modes are isotropic.

### 1.2 Formation of Non-Spherical Nuclei

When the nucleon count deviates from magic numbers, the shell is not completely filled. The node distribution on the nuclear surface becomes uneven:

- **Convex regions**: node density is high, nodes protrude outward;
- **Concave regions**: node density is low, nodes retract inward.

The nucleus takes on non-spherical shapes such as ellipsoidal, oblate, or pear-shaped.

### 1.3 The N2T Geometric Origin of Nuclear Shape

In the N2T framework, nuclear shape is directly determined by the spatial distribution of nuclear surface nodes. Regions with high node density produce "convexities," while regions with low node density produce "concavities." This convex-concave distribution is a geometric necessity when stacking nucleon three-dimensional fan-blade structures—when fan-blade directions fail to fully complement each other in certain directions, local node density deviates from the average.

## II. Connections Between Nuclear Surface Nodes and Electron Shells

### 2.1 Mode of Radial Edge Connections

Nuclear surface nodes establish connections with nodes of the shell-layer hexagonal grid through radial edges. Each radial edge carries the nuclear phase perturbation and transmits it to the shell-layer nodes.

- **Convex nodes (protruding regions)**：radial edges are shorter, phase coupling is stronger;
- **Concave nodes (recessed regions)**：radial edges are longer, phase coupling is weaker.

### 2.2 Superposition of Phase Perturbations

Phase perturbations carried by radial edges from different regions of the nuclear surface superimpose on the shell-layer hexagonal grid:

- Strong perturbations from multiple convex regions superimpose $\rightarrow$ local shell-layer node phase amplitudes increase;
- Weak perturbations from concave regions $\rightarrow$ local shell-layer node phase amplitudes decrease;
- Alternating distribution of convexities and concavities $\rightarrow$ multi-pole distortions appear in the shell-layer standing-wave modes.

## III. Distortion of Electron Shell Standing Waves

### 3.1 Electron Shells in Spherical Nuclei

When the nucleus is spherical, the nuclear surface nodes are uniformly distributed, radial edge lengths are similar in all directions, and the phase perturbation intensity is balanced across all directions. The shell-layer grid receives isotropic phase perturbations, so the standing-wave modes exhibit spherical symmetry (s orbitals) or standard multi-pole symmetry (p, d, f orbitals).

### 3.2 Electron Shells in Deformed Nuclei

When the nucleus is non-spherical, the convex-concave distribution causes non-uniform phase perturbations across directions:

| Nuclear Surface Feature | Effect on Shell-Layer Standing Waves | Macroscopic Manifestation |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Convex regions (dense nodes) | Shorter radial edges, stronger phase perturbations | Electron cloud is "pushed" outward in that direction |
| Concave regions (sparse nodes) | Longer radial edges, weaker phase perturbations | Electron cloud is "pulled" inward in that direction |
| Multi-pole convex-concave | Multi-pole phase perturbation superposition | Electron cloud exhibits higher-order distortions |
| Overall deviation from spherical | Overall asymmetric phase perturbation | Electron cloud overall deviates from spherical symmetry |

### 3.3 Progression of Distortion

The more severe the nuclear deformation, the more obvious the electron shell distortion:

- Slight deformation $\rightarrow$ electron cloud exhibits small quadrupole distortion (corresponding to electric quadrupole moment);
- Moderate deformation $\rightarrow$ electron cloud shape couples with nuclear shape, producing spectral line splitting;
- Severe deformation $\rightarrow$ electron shell standing-wave modes resonate with nuclear shape, producing complex chemical behavior.

## V. Conclusion

In the N2T framework, the nucleus is not necessarily spherical. When the nucleon count deviates from magic numbers, the nuclear surface node density distribution becomes uneven, forming convex and concave surfaces:

- **Convex regions**: dense nodes, shorter radial edges, stronger phase coupling;
- **Concave regions**: sparse nodes, longer radial edges, weaker phase coupling.

Phase perturbations from convex and concave nodes are transmitted through radial edges to the shell-layer hexagonal grid, and their superposition causes distortions in the electron shell standing-wave modes.

These distortions correspond to phenomena such as nuclear deformation, electric quadrupole moment, hyperfine structure, and isotope shift in standard physics. In the N2T framework, these effects are unifiedly explained as: **the superposition results of phase perturbations from convex and concave nuclear surface nodes on the shell-layer grid.**

**Nuclear shape—whether spherical or deformed—is not an isolated nuclear physics problem, but a geometric constraint that directly affects electron shell structure.**
