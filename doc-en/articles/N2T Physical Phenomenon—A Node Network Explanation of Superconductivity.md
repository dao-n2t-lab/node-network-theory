## I. The Principle of Superconducting Formation

### 1.1 Ordinary Conductors: Scattering of Traveling Waves

In ordinary conductors, electron standing waves moving along the lattice node network continuously undergo phase coupling with the lattice's thermal vibrations (phonons, i.e., traveling waves of nodal thermal vibration):

- Each coupling causes part of the energy to be scattered and converted into thermal vibration (resistance);
- The higher the temperature, the greater the thermal vibration amplitude $\rightarrow$ the more frequent the scattering $\rightarrow$ the greater the resistance.

### 1.2 The Superconducting State: Establishment of a Global Standing-Wave Network

When the material's temperature drops below the critical temperature $T_c$, the thermal vibration amplitude of the lattice node network drops to an extremely low level, and scattering nearly disappears. At this point:

- Electron standing waves that were originally moving independently begin to establish long-range phase coordination through the lattice node network;
- Two electron standing waves establish a phase-locked channel through the exchange of phonons (lattice vibrations)—this is the Cooper pair.

In this framework, a Cooper pair is not a "particle pair" but a "shared phase ring" formed by two electron standing waves through the lattice node network. This shared phase ring spans multiple lattice nodes, forming a macroscopic closed standing-wave path.

When the shared phase rings of a large number of Cooper pairs couple with each other, the entire superconductor forms a giant standing-wave network covering the entire material. In this network, the phase distribution of all electron standing waves is locked as a whole.

### 1.3 The Geometric Essence of Zero Resistance

In the superconducting state, when an external electric field (external phase gradient) acts on this giant standing-wave network:

- It does not push individual electron standing waves, but rather drives the phase distribution of the entire standing-wave network as a whole;
- Because there is no thermal vibration scattering of individual node clusters in the network, the change in this overall phase distribution is dissipationless;
- Macroscopically, this manifests as zero resistance.

In this framework, **zero resistance = phase-synchronized motion of the global standing-wave network, without local stress scattering**.

### 1.4 The Meissner Effect (Complete Diamagnetism)

Another key feature of superconductors is complete diamagnetism—external magnetic fields cannot penetrate the interior of the superconductor.

In this framework:

- When an external magnetic field (external phase gradient) attempts to enter the superconductor, it comes into phase conflict with the superconductor's global standing-wave network;
- To maintain internal phase synchronization, the surface node network of the superconductor spontaneously forms a phase ring of shielding current that counteracts the external phase gradient;
- The magnetic field generated by this surface phase ring is opposite in direction to the external magnetic field, making the internal magnetic field zero.

In this framework, **the Meissner effect = a phase shielding ring automatically formed on the superconductor's surface to protect the phase stability of the internal global standing-wave network**.

## II. Conditions for Forming Superconductivity

In the node network framework, the formation of the superconducting state requires three conditions to be satisfied simultaneously:

### Condition 1: The Phase Perturbation of Thermal Vibration Must Be Suppressed

At low temperatures, the thermal vibration amplitude of the lattice node network drops to an extremely low level, and phase noise nearly disappears. In this framework, this corresponds to:

- Lattice thermal vibration amplitude < node network phase locking threshold;
- When the temperature drops below the critical temperature $T_c$, the thermal vibration amplitude drops to a level insufficient to破坏 phase locking.

### Condition 2: The Phase Paths of Electron Standing Waves Must Be Highly Connected

Superconductivity requires that electron standing waves can find an unblocked global phase path in the material's node network. This requires:

- High-symmetry lattice topology (e.g., face-centered cubic, hexagonal honeycomb structures);
- Small atomic spacing, so that the wavelengths of electron standing waves can match the lattice node spacing (satisfying standing-wave conditions);
- Low defect density, so that phase paths are unobstructed.

In the framework, superconducting materials require that edges in the node network form aligned paths in multiple directions, minimizing phase conversion and frequency interference when traveling waves propagate.

### Condition 3: There Must Be a Sufficient Phase Locking Threshold

In low-temperature superconductivity, this threshold is provided by phonon-mediated Cooper pairs; in high-temperature superconductivity, it may be provided by other mechanisms (such as spin fluctuations or charge density waves).

In the framework, a higher phase locking threshold means the material can maintain the superconducting state at higher temperatures.

### The Unified Criterion for Superconducting Formation

Combining the above three conditions, the unified criterion for superconducting formation is:

$$\text{Superconductivity} \Leftrightarrow T < T_c \;\text{and}\; \rho_{\text{connectivity}} > \rho_{\text{threshold}} \;\text{and}\; \sigma_{\text{phase locking}} > \sigma_{\text{thermal noise}}$$

where:

- $T_c$ is the critical temperature (determined by the node network's natural frequency);
- $\rho_{\text{connectivity}}$ is the edge connection density in the node network;
- $\sigma_{\text{phase locking}}$ is the phase locking threshold of the node network;
- $\sigma_{\text{thermal noise}}$ is the phase noise generated by thermal vibration.

## III. Research Directions for Superconducting Materials

Based on the derivations of this framework, the development of superconducting materials can be突破 from the following directions:

### Direction 1: Search for Light-Element Materials with High Debye Temperatures

**Framework prediction**: If the natural frequency of the lattice node network is much higher than the frequency of thermal vibration at room temperature, the thermal vibration will "fail to keep up" with the speed of network phase adjustment, and its disruptive effect on global phase locking will be greatly reduced.

**Experimental path**: Carbon-based materials (diamond, graphene, carbon nanotubes), boron-based, and amino compounds have extremely high Debye temperatures (> 1000 K) and are candidates for room-temperature superconductivity.

### Direction 2: Design "Single-Mode Node Networks" — An Analogy from Optical Fibers to Superconductivity

**Framework prediction**: The ideal superconducting material should be like a single-mode optical fiber, supporting only one traveling-wave mode—forming a single phase-locked channel in a specific direction, with connections in other directions suppressed.

**Experimental path**:

- Artificial superlattices: constructing "core-cladding" structures at the atomic scale through atomic layer deposition, molecular beam epitaxy, and other techniques;
- Topological insulator/superconductor heterojunctions: using topological surface states as "single-mode channels" to confine superconducting phase locking to two-dimensional interfaces;
- Carbon nanotubes/graphene nanoribbons: quasi-one-dimensional node networks, naturally close to "single-mode" conditions.

### Direction 3: Compress Lattice Constants with High Pressure

**Framework prediction**: High-pressure compression of lattice node spacing brings the node network closer to the "single-mode" state, reducing phase conversion and frequency interference during traveling-wave propagation.

**Experimental path**: Hydride high-pressure superconductors (such as $\mathrm{H_3S}$, $\mathrm{LaH_{10}}$) have already achieved superconducting transition temperatures close to room temperature (~250 K) under high pressure.

### Direction 4: Doping and Interface Engineering

**Framework prediction**: Doping introduces additional edge connection density, enhancing the connectivity and robustness of the global phase network; interface engineering can suppress connections in other directions, bringing the phase network closer to the "single-mode" condition.

**Experimental path**: Doping optimization of copper oxide high-temperature superconductors (YBCO, Bi-2212), and interface regulation of iron-based superconductors.
