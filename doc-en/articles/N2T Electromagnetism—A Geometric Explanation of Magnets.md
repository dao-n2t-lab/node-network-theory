## I. Single Atom's Magnetic Moment: Microscopic Phase Difference

In the N2T framework, there exist closed loops (electron standing waves) in each atom's shell-layer node network. The topological phase $\Theta$ (the loop integral of phase factors on edges) defines the atom's "magnetic moment."

| Atom Type | Topological Phase $\Theta$ | Magnetic Moment State |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Iron, Cobalt, Nickel | Non-zero | Carries a net magnetic moment |
| Copper, Aluminum | Zero | No net magnetic moment |

In iron, cobalt, and nickel, the topological phase of the electron standing wave loop in the outer node network is non-zero, so each atom carries a tiny net phase difference—that is, an atomic magnetic moment. This is the microscopic source of magnetism.

## II. Formation of a Magnet: Mesoscopic Phase Synchronization

In an ordinary piece of iron, each atomic node cluster has a net topological phase, but the phase orientations are randomly distributed—macroscopically, the net magnetic moment is zero (non-magnetic).

When an iron block is placed in an external magnetic field (or is itself a permanent magnetic material), the formation of a magnet proceeds through four steps:

1. **External phase gradient action**: the external magnetic field (i.e., external phase gradient) acts on the iron block's node network, driving the topological phases of atomic node clusters to realign along the direction of the external phase gradient;
2. **Phase synchronization propagation**: adjacent atoms are connected through edges, and phase synchronization propagates through stress minimization—once a region's phase orientation is locked, it "induces" neighboring regions to synchronize;
3. **Magnetic domain formation**: regions of atoms with consistent phase orientation form magnetic domains. At the boundaries between domains, there exist phase-difference stresses;
4. **Macroscopic magnetization**: eventually, the phase orientations of a large number of atomic node clusters tend toward alignment, forming a macroscopic net topological phase—that is, a magnet.

In the framework, **a magnet is the macroscopic state in which the topological phases of a large number of atomic node clusters are aligned in the same direction.**

## III. Magnetic Field Lines: Macroscopic Phase Gradients and the Participation of External Nodes

The magnetic field lines around a magnet correspond, in the framework, to the distribution paths of phase gradients between the magnet's node network and the external spatial node network.

A key point needs to be clarified here: **magnetic field lines are not "emitted" by the magnet, but are phase distribution streamlines formed jointly by the magnet and external space nodes.** The external spatial node network is not "vacuum"—they are real nodes that exist, simply not forming material structures.

The formation process of magnetic field lines:

1. The phase orientation inside the magnet is aligned, producing a net phase difference from the N pole to the S pole;
2. This phase difference is transmitted to external space through edge connections between the magnet's surface nodes and external spatial nodes;
3. After receiving this phase difference, the external spatial node network adjusts its node phase distribution—forming phase gradients;
4. The distribution paths of phase gradients in external space are the magnetic field lines—extending from the high-phase region (N pole) to the low-phase region (S pole), forming closed paths.

**Magnetic field lines are the distribution paths of phase gradients formed jointly by the magnet's node network and the external spatial node network. Without the participation of external space nodes, the magnetic field cannot exist.**

## IV. Attraction and Repulsion: The Geometric Source of Macroscopic Forces

### Attraction

When two magnets approach each other, if their phase orientations are opposite (one N pole facing the other's S pole), the phase gradients can smoothly connect, reducing the system's total stress $\rightarrow$ generating a net attractive force.

### Repulsion

If their phase orientations are the same (N facing N), the phase gradients conflict, increasing the system's total stress $\rightarrow$ generating a net repulsive force.

**The essence of attraction and repulsion is the matching or mismatching of phase gradients—stress reduction leads to attraction; stress increase leads to repulsion.**

## V. External Space Nodes and the "Propagation" of the Magnetic Field

If there were no nodes in external space (i.e., a completely isolated magnet), the magnet's phase difference could not be transmitted outward, and there would be no phase gradients in external space—thus no magnetic field. In reality, external space always has a node network (albeit at extremely low density). Therefore, a magnetic field always exists around a magnet, because the magnet's phase difference can always be "extended" through the external node network.

This is consistent with the earlier discussion of "light propagating in vacuum":

- Light propagates in "vacuum" not by passing through emptiness, but by propagating through the external spatial node network;
- The existence of a magnetic field similarly depends on the participation of the external spatial node network.

**"Vacuum" is not empty—it is the manifestation of the node network in a low-density state.**
