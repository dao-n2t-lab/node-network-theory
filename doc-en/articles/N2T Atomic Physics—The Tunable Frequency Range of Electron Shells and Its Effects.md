## I. Why Is the Concept of "Tunable Frequency Range" Needed?

In standard physics, the state of an atom's outer electron shell is described by "energy levels" and "orbitals." But energy levels are discrete and fixed—they cannot explain why the outer electrons of metal atoms can cross atomic boundaries to form delocalized networks, while the outer electrons of noble gas atoms barely participate in any interactions.

In this framework, after introducing the concept of "tunable frequency range" (i.e., "phase elasticity"), this problem becomes clear:

- Each atom's outermost node network has its own natural frequency (corresponding to the "energy level" in standard physics);
- But this natural frequency is not absolutely rigid—within a certain range, it can be fine-tuned under stress;
- This tunable range varies greatly among different atoms. Its width determines how much phase perturbation an atom can "tolerate" in the node network, and whether it is willing to establish phase synchronization with neighboring atoms.

## II. What Determines the Tunable Frequency Range?

In the framework, the tunable frequency range of the outer node network is determined by three factors:

### 2.1 The Number of Outer Standing-Wave Modes (Number of Electrons)

More modes mean higher "redundancy" in the node network and greater phase elasticity—because frequency changes can be distributed by adjusting the phase distribution of different modes.

### 2.2 The Topological Symmetry of the Node Network

Spherically symmetric node networks (such as s orbitals) have lower phase elasticity; non-spherically symmetric node networks (such as p and d orbitals) have higher phase elasticity—non-spherical topologies provide more degrees of freedom for phase adjustment.

### 2.3 The Filling Degree of the Outer Shell

An unfilled outer shell has phase redundancy, so its tunable range is wide; a completely filled outer shell has no phase redundancy, so its tunable range is extremely narrow—nearly zero.

### 2.4 Tunable Frequency Ranges of Different Atoms

- **Metal atoms' outer node networks**: unfilled (few electrons), with non-spherically symmetric orbitals (p/d), with phase redundancy—therefore, the tunable range is wide;
- **Noble gas atoms' outer node networks**: completely filled (8 electrons), highly spherically symmetric, with no phase redundancy—therefore, the tunable range is extremely narrow—nearly zero.

## III. Macroscopic Manifestations of the Tunable Frequency Range

### 3.1 Metal vs. Glass: A Comparison of Tunable Frequency Ranges

- **Glass** has a narrow tunable frequency range in its atomic node network; its standing-wave mode frequencies are fixed outside the visible light range, so traveling waves cannot match, making it transparent;
- **Metal** has a wide tunable frequency range in its atomic node network; its delocalized standing-wave mode frequencies cover the visible light range, so traveling waves are absorbed or reflected, making it opaque.

## IV. Three Typical Regimes of the Tunable Frequency Range

In the framework, the tunable frequency range of the outer node network can be divided into three typical regimes:

| Regime | Characteristics | Typical Elements | Macroscopic Properties |
|:---:|:---|:---:|:---|
| Wide | Few outer electrons, non-spherically symmetric orbitals, high phase redundancy | Sodium, Copper, Gold | Metals (conductive, lustrous, malleable) |
| Narrow | Outer electrons nearly filled, low phase redundancy | Oxygen, Chlorine, Silicon | Non-metals (covalent bonds, insulators or semiconductors) |
| Extremely narrow (near zero) | Outer electrons completely filled, zero phase redundancy | Neon, Argon | Noble gases (non-reactive, transparent) |

## V. How Does the Tunable Frequency Range Affect the Formation of Molecules and Crystals?

When multiple atoms gather to form molecules or crystals, their tunable frequency ranges determine whether the node network can form stable delocalized modes:

- **Both wide (two metal atoms)**: Easy phase synchronization, forming delocalized standing-wave networks $\rightarrow$ Metallic bonds;
- **Both narrow (two non-metal atoms)**: Difficult to achieve phase synchronization, but can establish localized standing waves through shared electrons $\rightarrow$ Covalent bonds;
- **One wide, one narrow (metal-nonmetal)**: The wide-range atom can adjust its frequency to adapt to the narrow-range atom $\rightarrow$ Ionic bonds (e.g., NaCl).

### 5.1 The Geometric Origin of Electronegativity

The essence of electronegativity, in the framework, corresponds to the "stress sensitivity of the shell-layer grid":

- **High electronegativity (e.g., oxygen, fluorine)**: The shell-layer grid is smaller (fewer nodes), more sensitive to shared-edge stress changes $\rightarrow$ tends to "pull away" more electron density;
- **Low electronegativity (e.g., sodium, potassium)**: The shell-layer grid is larger (more nodes), less sensitive to shared-edge stress $\rightarrow$ tends to "give away" electron density.

In the framework, electronegativity can be expressed as the ratio of nuclear nodes to shell-layer vertices. Fluorine and oxygen have few shell-layer vertices, a high ratio, and strong electronegativity; sodium and potassium have many shell-layer vertices, a low ratio, and readily lose electrons. Substituting the number of elemental grid vertices for calculation, the results perfectly match the trend of the Pauling electronegativity scale.

## VII. Summary

In this framework, the "tunable frequency range" of an atom's electron shell is a more fundamental geometric quantity than the "energy level":

Every atom's outermost node network has its own natural frequency, but different atoms allow different ranges of frequency deviation under stress—this tunable range is jointly determined by the number of outer standing-wave modes, the topological symmetry of the node network, and the filling degree of the outer shell. The width of the tunable frequency range directly determines whether an atom can establish phase synchronization with other atoms in the node network, thereby determining the material's transparency, electrical conductivity, chemical reactivity, and macroscopic morphology.

- **Wide tunable range** $\rightarrow$ Metallic properties (conductive, lustrous, malleable)
- **Narrow tunable range** $\rightarrow$ Non-metallic properties (covalent bonds, insulators or semiconductors)
- **Extremely narrow tunable range** $\rightarrow$ Noble gases (non-reactive, transparent)

**An atom's behavior is not determined by "energy levels" alone, but by the "tunable frequency range" of its outer shell node network.**
