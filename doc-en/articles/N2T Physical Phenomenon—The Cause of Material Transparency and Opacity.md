## I. Phenomenon: Why Is Glass Transparent While Metal Is Opaque?

In everyday life, glass is transparent—we can see objects through it. Metals (such as copper, gold, and silver) are opaque—we cannot see what is behind them, and their surfaces have a luster.

In standard physics:

- **Glass is transparent**: because its electronic band gap is larger than visible light energy, photons cannot excite electron transitions;
- **Metal is opaque**: because its conduction band contains a large number of free electrons, visible light is absorbed or reflected.

In the N2T framework, these two explanations are unified into a single mechanism: **the matching between the frequency distribution of standing-wave modes in the node network and the frequency of traveling waves**.

## II. Why Does Glass's Node Network Have No Standing-Wave Modes in the Visible Light Range?

Glass's node network (SiO₂) consists of silicon and oxygen atoms connected by covalent bonds, forming an amorphous three-dimensional network. Its characteristics are:

- **Atomic spacing**: Si-O bond length is approximately 0.16 nm, O-O spacing is approximately 0.26 nm, far smaller than visible light wavelengths (380~780 nm);
- **Standing-wave mode distribution**:
  - Ultraviolet region (< 380 nm): Si-O bond electron transitions (standing-wave modes);
  - Infrared region (> 780 nm): Si-O bond stretching and bending vibrations (standing-wave modes);
  - **Visible light region (380~780 nm)**: No matchable standing-wave modes exist.

In the visible light range, glass's node network has no standing-wave modes that can "capture" traveling waves. Traveling waves cannot resonate with the network and can only propagate through the network without losing energy—manifesting macroscopically as transparency.

In the framework, **glass is transparent not because it is "empty," but because its node network does not "resonate" with visible light**.

## III. Why Does Metal's Node Network Have Abundant Standing-Wave Modes in the Visible Light Range?

Metal's node network (such as copper, gold, and silver) has characteristics completely different from glass:

- **Atomic spacing**: Smaller (copper ~0.36 nm), but the ratio to visible light wavelengths is comparable to that of glass;
- **Standing-wave mode distribution**:
  - Outer electrons (free electrons) in metals form delocalized standing-wave modes;
  - These delocalized modes span the entire material with a continuous frequency distribution;
  - **The visible light range (380~780 nm) happens to fall within this continuous distribution**.

Metal's node network has a large number of matchable standing-wave modes in the visible light range. ## IV. Why Can Metal's Electron Standing Waves Delocalize While Glass's Cannot?

The core difference lies in the phase coupling state of the outer electrons:

- **Metal atoms**: the standing-wave frequencies of outer electrons are close to those of neighboring atoms—they have a "common language," synchronize easily, and form delocalized networks covering the entire lattice;
- **Glass atoms**: the standing-wave frequencies of outer electrons differ greatly, lacking a "common language," so electron standing waves are localized at their respective positions.

## V. Why Does a Completely Filled Outer Shell Lead to "Phase Rigidity"?

In the framework, a filled electron shell means that all available standing-wave modes in that shell are already occupied, and the phase distribution is locked:

- **No vacancies**: no additional standing-wave modes can accommodate new electrons;
- **Fixed phase distribution**: the phase differences of all nodes have reached stress equilibrium and cannot be adjusted to match new frequencies;
- **Large frequency difference from neighboring atoms**: the natural frequency of a completely filled shell node network is high, differing greatly from the outer frequencies of other atoms, making synchronization difficult.

A completely filled shell node network is at the "stress equilibrium limit"—all phase degrees of freedom have been used up. Any attempt to change its state (such as increasing frequency or adding new electrons) will break the equilibrium and require external energy input.

## VI. The Unified Criterion for Transparency and Opacity

In the node network framework, the criterion for transparency and opacity can be unified as:

**Glass's standing-wave modes are outside the visible light range, so it is transparent; metal's standing-wave modes are within the visible light range, so it is opaque.**

The difference between the two does not come from "density" or "voids," but from "whether the frequency distribution of the node network's standing-wave modes overlaps with the frequency of traveling waves."
