In standard quantum mechanics, the shapes of electron orbitals (s, p, d, f) are given by solutions to the Schrödinger equation, and the number of angular nodal surfaces is a mathematical feature of the wavefunction. In the N2T framework, these orbital shapes are reinterpreted as: the projections of standing-wave modes on the hexagonal grid of the atomic shell layer in three-dimensional space.

This article presents the N2T framework's geometric explanation of electron orbital shapes from four perspectives: the structure of the shell-layer node network, the geometric meaning of angular nodal surfaces, the standing-wave modes of s/p/d/f orbitals, and the correspondence with standard quantum mechanics.

## I. The Shell-Layer Node Network as "Standing-Wave Space"

In the N2T framework, the outer shell of an atom is not an empty shell, but a closed spherical network composed of hexagonal grids (similar to a "honeycomb grid on a sphere").

The electron is not a particle "moving around the nucleus," but a standing-wave mode on this grid. The distribution of the standing wave on the grid is what we observe as the "electron cloud."

Standing-wave modes are determined by nodal surfaces in two dimensions:

- **Radial nodal surfaces**: vibration nodes along the radial direction (determining the principal quantum number $n$);
- **Angular nodal surfaces**: phase-flip interfaces along angular directions (determining the angular quantum number $l$).

This article focuses on angular nodal surfaces—they directly determine orbital shapes.

## II. The Geometric Definition of Angular Nodal Surfaces

### 2.1 What Is an Angular Nodal Surface?

An angular nodal surface is a geometric surface on the shell-layer grid where the vibration amplitude of the node network is zero.

It corresponds to: **the position where the node network undergoes a "phase flip" in the angular direction.**

In the node network, an angular nodal surface is not an "empty" region—it is a phase interface where the vibration amplitude is zero, with node phases on one side opposite to those on the other side.

### 2.2 Angular Nodal Surfaces in the Node Network

On a spherical surface, angular nodal surfaces manifest as:

- **s orbital**: no nodal surfaces—all nodes vibrate in phase, uniformly distributed on the sphere;
- **p orbital**: one nodal surface—along a certain axis, one side has positive phase and the other negative phase, forming an equatorial plane;
- **d orbital**: two nodal surfaces—alternating flips along two orthogonal directions, forming a "four-lobe" distribution;
- **f orbital**: three nodal surfaces—alternating flips along three orthogonal directions, forming a more complex "multi-lobe" distribution.

The number of angular nodal surfaces directly corresponds to the angular quantum number $l$ in quantum mechanics.

## III. Node Network Manifestations of the Four Orbital Shapes

### 3.1 s Orbital: Spherically Symmetric Mode

- **Number of angular nodal surfaces**: 0;
- **Node network vibration distribution**: all nodes vibrate in phase, completely uniformly distributed on the sphere;
- **Electron cloud shape**: spherically symmetric;
- **N2T mechanism**: the lowest-order spherical standing wave; all nodes vibrate synchronously, with amplitude decaying monotonically outward from the center.

The s orbital is the simplest mode among all orbitals—the shell-layer grid vibrates as a whole in synchronization.

### 3.2 p Orbital: Dipole Vibration Mode

- **Number of angular nodal surfaces**: 1;
- **Node network vibration distribution**: along a certain axis, the phase of nodes on one side is opposite to that on the other side;
- **Electron cloud shape**: dumbbell-shaped (stretched along a certain axis);
- **N2T mechanism**: the node network is "stretched" or "compressed" along a certain direction, forming two regions of nodes with opposite phases.

The three spatial orientations of p orbitals ($p_x$, $p_y$, $p_z$) correspond to the projections of the dipole direction onto the three spatial axes.

### 3.3 d Orbital: Quadrupole Vibration Mode

- **Number of angular nodal surfaces**: 2;
- **Node network vibration distribution**: node phases alternate along two orthogonal axes;
- **Electron cloud shape**: four-leaf clover shape (extended along two axes);
- **N2T mechanism**: the node network vibrates alternately in four directions in a regular pattern.

The five independent orientations of d orbitals ($d_{xy}$, $d_{xz}$, $d_{yz}$, $d_{x^2-y^2}$, $d_{z^2}$) correspond to different projections of the quadrupole mode in three-dimensional space.

### 3.4 f Orbital: Octupole Vibration Mode

- **Number of angular nodal surfaces**: 3;
- **Node network vibration distribution**: node phases form a more complex alternating pattern along three orthogonal axes;
- **Electron cloud shape**: more complex multi-lobe structure;
- **N2T mechanism**: the node network forms an octupole phase distribution in three-dimensional space.

The seven independent orientations of f orbitals correspond to different projections of the octupole mode in three-dimensional space.

## V. The Unified Origin of Orbital Shapes

In the N2T framework, the shapes of s, p, d, and f orbitals can all be traced back to the same origin:

**The standing-wave modes of the shell-layer node network on the three-dimensional spherical surface, where the number of angular nodal surfaces determines the symmetry of the orbital shape.**

- **s orbital**: no angular nodal surfaces, spherically symmetric;
- **p orbital**: one angular nodal surface, dumbbell-shaped;

**Orbital shapes are not the result of probability waves, but the projection in three-dimensional space of standing-wave modes naturally formed by the node network in a stress-balanced state.
