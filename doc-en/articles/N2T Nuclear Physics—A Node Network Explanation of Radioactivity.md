This article presents the N2T framework's geometric explanation of radioactivity from four perspectives: the unified mechanism of radioactivity, the node network correspondences of the three decay types, the geometric origin of half-life, and the correspondence with standard nuclear physics.

## I. The Unified Mechanism of Radioactivity: Stress-Driven Unlock

In the N2T framework, the atomic nucleus is a node cluster in stress balance. When internal stress exceeds the threshold, the node network cannot maintain its current topology and must release excess stress.

**Radioactivity is the "stress release" behavior of the nuclear node network under stress overload.**

### 1.1 Why Does Stress Overload Occur?

Internal stress in the nucleus comes from:

- Compressive stress on strong-force edges between nucleons;
- Accumulation of phase stress between protons (the geometric version of Coulomb pressure);
- Deviation of the internal-to-external stress ratio as the total number of nucleons increases.

When the sum of these stresses exceeds the threshold that the node network can sustain, the nucleus must undergo topological restructuring—releasing stress and restoring stress balance.

### 1.2 A Unified Framework for the Three Decay Types

In the node network, stress can be released in three fundamental ways:

| Decay Type | Stress Release Mode | Released Products |
|:---|:---|:---|
| α decay | Local node cluster detaches from parent nucleus | Helium nucleus node cluster (4 nucleons) |
| β decay | Quark ring phase flip, nucleon identity rewriting | Electron/positron + neutrino |
| γ decay | Standing-wave mode transition of the shell-layer grid | Photon (traveling wave) |

These represent three unlock paths of the node network under different stress states.

## II. α Decay: Detachment of a Local Node Cluster

### 2.1 The α Particle in the Node Network

In the N2T framework, the α particle is a stable tetrahedral node cluster composed of 4 nucleons (i.e., a helium nucleus). It is the smallest independently existing nucleon cluster.

Inside a heavy nucleus, nucleons in certain regions may form a pre-formed α cluster. When the internal stress in that region exceeds the threshold, the shared edges between the α cluster and the parent nucleus break, and the entire cluster is ejected.

### 2.2 The Network Mechanism of α Decay

1. The edge stress around a certain tetrahedral node cluster (α particle) inside the nucleus gradually increases;
2. When the stress exceeds the threshold, the strong-force edges between the α cluster and the parent nucleus break;
3. The α cluster gains kinetic energy for outward motion and is ejected from the parent nucleus;
4. The remaining node network of the parent nucleus readjusts its topology, entering a new stress equilibrium state.

### 2.3 Why Does α Decay Occur in Heavy Nuclei?

In heavy nuclei, the large number of nucleons leads to uneven stress distribution among them. Stress accumulation in certain regions exceeds the threshold, forming "pre-formed α clusters." The unevenness of stress is higher in heavy nuclei, making them more prone to α decay.

**α decay is the process by which a heavy nucleus node network releases stress by breaking edges and ejecting a stable sub-cluster when local stress is overloaded.**

## III. β Decay: Phase Flip of Quark Rings

### 3.1 The Node Network Mechanism of β Decay

β decay corresponds to a topological phase flip of a quark ring inside a nucleon (neutron or proton) within the nucleus.

In the node network, a neutron's quark ring composition is (u,d,d). When the topological phase $\Theta$ of a down quark ring flips, it becomes an up quark ring, and the neutron transforms into a proton:

$$udd \rightarrow uud + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$

### 3.2 How Does the Phase Flip Produce an Electron?

When the quark ring phase flips, the released phase stress propagates outward in the form of a traveling wave along the radial edges of the node network. When this traveling wave reaches the shell-layer grid, it is "locked" as a standing-wave mode of a hexagonal ring—this produces an electron.

Meanwhile, a small portion of the stress not locked in the phase flip propagates outward as an extremely low-frequency, weakly coupled traveling wave—this is the neutrino.

### 3.3 Why Is β Decay a "Weak Interaction"?

In the node network, the phase flip of a quark ring must cross an energy barrier. The height of this barrier determines the probability of decay—corresponding in phenomenon to the "weak interaction" strength in the standard model.

But the N2T framework does not need to introduce the "weak interaction" as an independent force—it is merely the statistical manifestation of quark ring phase flips.

**β decay is the rewriting of nucleon identity caused by the phase flip of internal quark rings, with the released phase stress converting into an electron and a neutrino.**

## IV. γ Decay: Mode Transition of the Shell-Layer Grid

### 4.1 The Node Network Mechanism of γ Decay

γ decay corresponds to a jump in the order of the collective vibration mode of the nucleus.

The nucleus, as a node cluster, has overall vibration modes (similar to a "nuclear breathing mode"). When the nucleus returns from an excited state to the ground state, the standing-wave mode jumps from a higher order to a lower order, and the released energy propagates as a traveling wave.

This traveling wave propagates along the nuclear shell-layer grid and then leaves the nucleus into free space—macroscopically appearing as a γ photon.

### 4.2 Differences Between γ Decay and α, β Decay

| Decay Type | Network Behavior | Released Products | Stress Source |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| α decay | Local node cluster detachment | Helium nucleus | Local stress overload |
| β decay | Quark ring phase flip | Electron + neutrino | Intra-nucleon phase stress |
| γ decay | Standing-wave mode transition | Photon | Collective vibration mode order change |

### 4.3 The Relationship Between γ Decay and Photons

In the N2T framework, a photon is a traveling wave—vibrational energy propagating along open paths. The photon in γ decay is not a "particle" but a traveling wave released from the transition of standing-wave modes in the node network.

**γ decay is the traveling wave energy released by a transition in the nuclear standing-wave mode order.**

## V. The Geometric Origin of Half-Life

In standard physics, half-life is a probabilistic concept—each nuclide has the same probability of decaying, and it is impossible to predict when a single nucleus will decay. In the N2T framework, half-life reflects the statistical waiting time required for stress within the nuclear node network to accumulate to the unlock threshold.

### 5.1 The Randomness of Stress Accumulation

In the node network, stress is not uniform:

- Quantum fluctuations (phase noise) cause local stress to constantly fluctuate;
- Phase differences between nodes continuously fine-tune, generating random stress distributions;
- When stress in a region accidentally exceeds the threshold, decay occurs.

### 5.2 Determinants of Half-Life

The length of the half-life depends on:

| Factor | Effect on Half-Life |
|:---|:---|
| Total number of nucleons | More nucleons $\rightarrow$ more uneven stress distribution $\rightarrow$ higher decay probability |
| Stress threshold | Higher threshold $\rightarrow$ longer stress accumulation needed $\rightarrow$ longer half-life |
| Phase noise intensity | Stronger noise $\rightarrow$ faster unlock triggering $\rightarrow$ shorter half-life |
| Proximity to magic numbers | Closer to magic numbers (perfectly symmetric structures) $\rightarrow$ greater stress margin $\rightarrow$ longer half-life |

### 5.3 Framework-Predicted Half-Life Relationship

In the N2T framework, the half-life can be approximately expressed as:

$$T_{1/2} \propto \frac{\sigma_{\text{threshold}}^2}{\langle \Delta \sigma^2 \rangle}$$

That is: half-life is proportional to the square of the stress threshold and inversely proportional to the variance of phase noise. This relationship requires additional assumptions in the standard model, but in the N2T framework it is directly derived from the stress statistics of the node network.
