## I. Node Density Fluctuations: The Seeds of Structure Formation

### 1.1 The Early Universe: Approximately Uniform Node Density

In the early universe, the node density in the node network was approximately uniform across the observable universe. At this time, nodes were in a low-stress state, with edge lengths close to their natural length, and no significant structures had yet formed.

But this "uniformity" was not absolute—due to the finiteness of the number of nodes and the randomness of the initial phase distribution, there existed small fluctuations in node density.

### 1.2 Density Fluctuations $\rightarrow$ Stress Gradients

In the N2T framework, any non-uniform distribution of node density generates stress gradients:

$$\nabla S \propto -\nabla \rho_{\text{node}}$$

Regions with high density generate local high stress, while regions with low density generate local low stress. These stress gradients drive node clusters to move from high-density regions to low-density regions—that is, node density fluctuations are amplified by stress gradients.

### 1.3 Positive Feedback Cycle

When the node density in a region is slightly higher than in surrounding areas:

1. The region generates a weak stress gradient;
2. Neighboring nodes move toward the region, further increasing the density;
3. The increased density further strengthens the stress gradient;
4. The cycle continues, eventually forming a high-density node cluster.

This is the "structure formation" mechanism in the N2T framework—it does not rely on the gravitational collapse of dark matter, but on the self-amplifying process of node density fluctuations.

## II. Structure Formation: From Node Clusters to Galaxies

### 2.1 The Hierarchical Structure of Node Clusters

In the self-amplifying process of node density, the scale of node clusters expands step by step:

| Scale | Structure | Node Density Characteristic |
|:---|:---|:---|
| $10^6$ light-years | Galaxy groups/clusters | Medium-high density regions |
| $10^8$ light-years | Superclusters | Medium density regions |
| $10^9$ light-years | Cosmic web (filamentary structure) | High-density "filaments" interwoven with low-density "voids" |
| $> 10^{10}$ light-years | Cosmic voids | Extremely low node density |

Each level of structure comes from the cumulative amplification of node density gradients at larger scales.

### 2.2 Galaxy Formation: Local Node Density Peaks

At galactic scales, node density reaches local peaks. These high-density regions manifest as:

- Edges between nodes are compressed, stress increases $\rightarrow$ mass concentration;
- The node density gradients in high-density regions generate local "gravitational wells";
- Surrounding nodes are attracted and locked into high-density regions $\rightarrow$ galaxy formation.

In the N2T framework, **galaxies are not formed by the "condensation" of dark matter, but are a natural product of the self-amplifying process of node density fluctuations.**

### 2.3 Galaxy Rotation Curves and Node Density Distribution

Observed galaxy rotation curves show that the rotation speed at the galaxy's outskirts does not decrease with distance (the standard model explains this with dark matter).

In the N2T framework, this phenomenon corresponds to:

- Although the node density in the outskirts of a galaxy is lower than at the center, it is not zero;
- Stress gradients in the peripheral node network still exist, exerting additional centripetal drag on visible matter;
- This drag keeps the rotation curve flat—no dark matter particles need to be introduced.

**The "extra gravity" at galactic outskirts comes from the long-range distribution of node density, not from hypothetical unknown particles.**

## III. The Cosmic Web and Voids: The Polarization of Node Density

### 3.1 The Cosmic Web: High-Density Filamentary Structures

In the N2T framework, the filamentary structure of the cosmic web corresponds to high-density connected paths in the node network:

- Node density forms continuous "ridges" along certain directions;
- These ridges manifest macroscopically as the filamentary structure of superclusters;
- The node network in these regions has higher stress accumulation and phase coupling.

### 3.2 Cosmic Voids: Extremely Low-Density Regions

Cosmic voids correspond to extremely low-density regions of the node network:

- The average distance between nodes is much larger than the natural length;
- Edges are in a very weak locking state, almost stress-free;
- The node density gradient inside voids is nearly zero, so there is almost no gravitational effect.

In the N2T framework, **voids are not "hollowed out," but are the natural result of nodes never having been attracted to those regions.**

### 3.3 The Shape of Voids and Stress Distribution

The shape of cosmic voids (approximately ellipsoidal or anisotropic) reflects the directionality of node density gradients:

- The long axis of a void corresponds to paths with weaker node density gradients;
- The short axis of a void corresponds to directions with stronger node density gradients (that is, the direction of matter accumulation).

**The shape of voids is the geometric imprint of stress gradients in the node network.**

$$\text{Node density fluctuations} + \text{Stress gradient driving} \rightarrow \text{Positive feedback cycle} \rightarrow \text{Progressive structure formation}$$

**The universe is not composed of "dark matter," but of "non-uniformly distributed nodes."**
