### Abstract

This paper proposes the Node‑Network Theory (N2T), a physical model based on discrete structures and stress minimization. The theory holds that the underlying universe is composed of nodes and edges. Nodes generate waves through vibration; phase differences of waves drive stress, and stress drives nodes to rearrange, hierarchically emerging all structures ranging from elementary particles to the macroscopic cosmos. N2T presupposes only three fundamental parameters — total number of nodes $N_{0}$, initial average spacing $d_{0}$, and initial vibration frequency $\omega_{0}$. It is calibrated by five observational values (Bohr radius, fine‑structure constant, electron mass, total mass of the observable universe, average nucleon mass). After calibration, all other constants emerge naturally from network geometry without additional independent presuppositions. This paper is an open‑source thought‑experiment, demonstrating a feasible path for deriving cosmic complexity from minimal underlying assumptions. Several open questions remain within the framework, including the geometric mechanism of quantum entanglement, emergence of the arrow of time, and the precise isomorphism between Lie groups and network topology, which await further research and refinement.

### 4.1 Nodes and Edges: the "Source Code" of the Universe

We may understand it this way: if we keep decomposing objects in the universe, what we finally obtain will no longer be "matter", but nodes without mass, without size, and without internal structure.

Yet if nodes remain completely inactive, the universe will stay static and unchanged. To "set the universe in motion", nodes must possess some fundamental activity. That activity shall be vibration, because only vibration can simultaneously produce the following properties:

- Period and phase — enabling synchronization or phase‑offset among nodes;
- Positive and negative values — generating directional attraction or repulsion;
- Amplitude and range — determining interaction strength and effective distance;
- Gradation and propagation — allowing interactions to travel, attenuate and superimpose.

These properties together constitute the full possibilities for interactions between nodes. Without vibration there would be no phase, no waves, no forces, no structures — and therefore no universe. Hence vibration is the sole pre‑assigned activity for nodes, and all complex cosmic phenomena originate from it.

(To demand further division beyond this point would sink into nihilism.)

At the birth of the universe, nodes are brought into existence "out of nothing", and endowed with the basic interaction engine: generating waves via vibration. Attraction and repulsion arise from wave phase (positive / negative), enabling self‑organized formation of connections. The minimum stable distance between two nodes equals half‑wavelength ($\lambda / 2$).

Two forms of waves are distinguished:

- **Standing wave**: locked vibration patterns circulating on closed paths, corresponding to particles such as quarks, electrons and protons.
- **Travelling wave**: vibration patterns propagating freely along open paths, corresponding to photons, radio waves, etc.

In the node‑network model, the node‑network itself constitutes space; there exists no separate external stage. Node‑vibration generates spatial potential. Mathematically this potential has no upper bound and decays following roughly $1/r^{2}$, never reaching zero. Physically its effective range is determined by node‑network density, stress state and phase‑coupling strength.

**Step 1: Points and vibration emerging "from nothing"**  
A node may be regarded as an event or a degree‑of‑freedom in space. In its initial silent state it carries no mass or charge, only potential vibration amplitude $a_{i}(t)$ and phase $\phi_{i}(t)$.  
Vibration source: each node receives an initial perturbation, like a stone thrown onto still water. Phases and amplitudes may be random, or originate from a unified initial pulse.  
Wave generation: once a node vibrates ($a_{i}\sin(\omega t+\phi_{i})$), it radiates waves outwards.  
This represents the first act of cosmic creation: transitioning from silence to pulsation, and the birth of information.

**Step 2: Wave propagation and attenuation in space**  
Propagation mechanism: waves travel outward from source points along all possible directions (within discrete networks: along every edge).  
Attenuation mechanism: wave intensity decreases with distance. In three‑dimensional space it decays as $1/r$ or $1/r^{2}$; in discrete networks it may be defined as exponential decay $e^{-\lambda L_{ij}}$ with respect to edge‑count or geometric distance.  
Wave form: a function of space and time $\Psi(x,t)$ carrying both amplitude and phase information.  
This forms the information‑transfer mechanism: waves replace transmitters; space itself acts as wave medium.

**Step 3: Positive‑/negative wave phase → attraction and repulsion**  
Positive / negative refers to the instantaneous phase direction of a wave, i.e. whether $\sin(\omega t+\phi)$ is greater or less than zero. This is not a fixed intrinsic property but dynamically variable.  
Interaction rule: after nodes "observe" waves from neighbours, instantaneous phase produces force: same‑phase → attraction (tendency to synchronize vibration); opposite‑phase → repulsion (tendency for destructive interference).  
This mechanism naturally realizes "like repels like, opposites attract". Here "like" means instantaneous phase‑relationship rather than fixed charge labels.

**Step 4: Force → formation and evolution of connections**  
Connection criterion: when sustained attraction arises due to phase‑synchronization, nodes draw closer (edge‑length decreases).  
Connection establishment: when approaching below a threshold value, permanent edges are created, recording phase‑difference, distance and coupling strength.  
Strengthening / weakening of connections: sustained phase‑synchronization increases edge weight; changing phase‑relations weaken edges or even break them completely.  
"Nodes are defined by their relations": connections are not pre‑existing; they are dynamically generated and maintained through vibrational interactions of nodes.

Stable connections between nodes require three simultaneous conditions: matching of initial spacing and frequency allowing waves to reach each‑other; balance between attraction from phase‑synchronization and compression‑repulsion from waves; vibration‑amplitude settling at a stress‑minimum state. These three conditions together determine connection rules of the node‑network, upon which all higher‑level structures are built.

Calculations consist of three components:

- Stress‑function: defines current degree of instability (target quantity to be minimized)
- Evolution‑rules: defines how nodes respond to stress and move toward stable states
- Convergence‑conditions: defines when stability is reached and adjustment may halt

**Stress‑function $S$**  
Total stress contains two terms: phase‑stress $S_{\theta}$ originating from phase‑differences of adjacent nodes; geometric‑stress $S_{g}$ originating from deviations of edge‑length from natural length $L_{0}$.

$$S=\sum_{< i,j>}\Big[\alpha\left(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}-\gamma_{ij}\right)^{2}+\beta\left(L_{ij}-L_{0}\right)^{2}\Big]$$

- $\theta_{i}$: phase of node $i$
- $\gamma_{ij}$: phase‑factor on edge $(i,j)$
- $L_{ij}$: actual edge‑length
- $L_{0}$: natural length
- $\alpha$, $\beta$: coupling coefficients

Smaller total stress $S$ corresponds to more stable structures. Evolving toward stability means gradually reducing $S$.

**Evolution‑rules**  
Phase‑update for each node:

$$\theta_{i}^{(t+1)}=\theta_{i}^{(t)}-\eta_{\theta}\cdot\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta_{i}}$$

Edge‑length update for every edge:

$$L_{ij}^{(t+1)}=L_{ij}^{(t)}-\eta_{L}\cdot\frac{\partial S}{\partial L_{ij}}$$

Where $\eta_{\theta}$ and $\eta_{L}$ represent update step‑sizes.  
These two formulas express that node‑phases and edge‑lengths change along directions of steepest descent for total stress — the mathematical formulation for "choosing the locally best available state".

**Convergence‑condition for stability**  
The system reaches stable equilibrium when:

$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta_{i}}=0,\quad \frac{\partial S}{\partial L_{ij}}=0$$

Gradients for all node‑phases and edge‑lengths vanish; the system sits at a stress‑minimum point. For the "shared‑central‑node quark‑ring" structure described earlier, these convergence‑conditions naturally yield: phase‑differences locked at $120^\circ$ among three quark‑rings; equal radial‑edge lengths; stable hexagonal or quadrilateral connections among outer‑perimeter nodes.

The three phase‑values correspond to red‑green‑blue color‑charges; summing‑to‑360° realizes color‑neutral states. This reproduces quark‑color‑symmetry without artificial numerical fitting.

Hadrons assembled from two‑ or three‑quarks:

Now composite triangular‑ring complexes are assembled into observable elementary particles: protons, neutrons, electrons and neutrinos.

**Neutrinos**: Neutrinos are not built from fixed‑counts of nodes; their geometry differs fundamentally from quark triangular‑loops and electron hexagonal‑loops. - Two‑node open‑chain: two‑nodes linked by weak‑coupling‑edge forming open non‑closed structures capable of flipping along the time‑direction (Majorana‑like properties) with almost zero Higgs‑field‑coupling.

Neutrinos carry no charge and self‑balance their phase‑stress; they may adopt simpler open‑chain or soliton‑geometries without requiring compensation‑closed‑loops. Essentially neutrinos represent topological‑defects propagating through weakly‑coupled networks with nearly‑absent closed‑loops. Their tiny masses originate from transient strain‑perturbations of regions they traverse. Single‑node‑solitons and two‑node open‑chains constitute base topologies; phase‑mixing between these two topologies generates flavor‑eigenstates for three neutrino‑generations. Topological phase‑differences along propagation‑paths produce neutrino‑oscillation‑phenomena.

Phenomena explainable in this subsection:

### 4.5 Molecules: atoms sharing outer‑shell grids

When two atoms approach each‑other, their outer‑shell hexagonal‑grids couple through edge‑sharing and form molecules.

Condition for edge‑sharing: phase‑matching on shared‑edges (zero phase‑difference across shared‑edge).  
Consequence of edge‑sharing: atomic‑nucleus vibration‑phases couple through shared‑edges into larger resonant‑systems, yielding collective molecular vibrational‑modes.

Bond‑strength proportional to quantity and topology of shared‑edges:

- Single‑bond: share 1 edge (connecting two‑nodes).
- Double‑bond: share 2 edges (connecting four‑nodes).
- Triple‑bond: share 3 edges (connecting six‑nodes).

Bond‑strength scales with stress‑coupling‑coefficient determined by shared‑edge‑length and phase‑matching‑quality.

**Molecular‑orbitals = standing‑wave‑modes on merged outer‑shell‑grids**  
After outer‑shell‑grids share edges, the combined outer‑shell becomes one larger hexagonal‑network.

- Bonding‑orbital: in‑phase superposition → electron‑density concentrates around shared‑edge‑regions ("chemical‑bond"); total phase‑stress decreases and energy is released.
- Antibonding‑orbital: anti‑phase superposition → electron‑density avoids shared‑edge‑regions ("antibond"); total phase‑stress rises and energy‑input is required.

Bond‑strength is proportional to number of shared‑edges and phase‑matching‑coupling‑coefficient. Total‑stress‑differences allow direct computation of covalent‑bond‑energies and minimum‑stress molecular‑geometries (for example the $104.5^\circ$ bond‑angle for water‑molecules).

**Electronegativity = stress‑sensitivity of outer‑shell‑grids**

Low‑electronegativity elements such as sodium and potassium feature larger outer‑shell‑grids with many vertices, lower stress‑sensitivity and tend to donate electrons. Within this framework electronegativity equals ratio: $N_{\mathrm{nuc}} / N_{\mathrm{shell‑vertex}}$. For fluorine / oxygen this ratio is large giving high electronegativity; sodium / potassium yield small‑ratios favouring electron‑donation. Calculations using grid‑vertex‑counts reproduce trends from Pauling‑electronegativity‑scale, confirming this ratio possesses quantitative explanatory‑power for chemistry.

**Chemical‑reactions = reconstruction of outer‑shell‑grids**

1. Two outer‑shell‑grids couple via shared‑edges.
2. Following stress‑minimization‑principles some edges break and new edges form through redistribution.
3. Electron standing‑wave‑modes transform from atomic‑orbitals into molecular‑orbitals.

Phenomena explainable in this subsection:

### 7.1 Natural Cut‑off for the Vacuum‑Energy Catastrophe

Recall the dilemma in traditional quantum‑field‑theory: under the assumption of continuous space, infinitely many vibrational modes yield a theoretical vacuum‑energy density of $10^{113}\ \mathrm{J/m^{3}}$, while astronomical observations give about $10^{-9}\ \mathrm{J/m^{3}}$. There exists a huge $10^{122}$‑fold discrepancy, known as the vacuum‑energy catastrophe in physics.

Derivation within this framework:

Space is represented as a discrete node‑network with finite total nodes $N_{0}\approx 10^{80}$. Accordingly, the total number of accessible vibrational modes is finite. The highest effective frequency is truncated by the minimal stable distance:

$$l_{\mathrm{min}}=d_{0}\cdot\alpha^{2}\approx 2.82\times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{m}$$

$$\omega_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{c}{l_{\mathrm{min}}}\approx 1.06\times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$

Zero‑point energy per node is approximately $\frac12\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{max}} \approx 5\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{J}$.

Total zero‑point energy across the observable universe:

$$E_{\mathrm{vac}} \approx N_{0}\cdot \frac12 \hbar\omega_{\mathrm{max}} \approx 10^{68}\ \mathrm{J}$$

Distributed over observable‑universe volume $V_{\mathrm{obs}}\approx 10^{80}\ \mathrm{m^{3}}$, we obtain vacuum‑energy density:

$$\rho_{\mathrm{vac}} \approx \frac{E_{\mathrm{vac}}}{V_{\mathrm{obs}}} \approx 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{J/m^{3}}$$

After introducing the hexagonal closed‑shell geometric correction factor (roughly $4\pi\times137\approx 1720$), the value can approach $\approx 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{J/m^{3}}$, consistent with observational order‑of‑magnitude.

### 9.2 Unified Modeling of All Objects

Every object possesses the complete six‑layer structure:

1. **Component layer**: composed of physical objects.
2. **Architecture layer**: physical connections among components.
3. **Mechanism layer**: standardized combinations of interaction modes between components.
4. **Transaction layer**: one complete interaction among components.
5. **Event layer**: one concrete instance of a transaction.
6. **Information layer**: material carriers and their states‑attributes transmitted through interactions.

Mechanisms support the formation of complete internal operation and external interaction patterns. Transactions can be driven by periodic rhythms or trigger‑conditions; nesting and invocation are supported.

#### Passive six‑layer structure for purely‑physical objects

Particles, atoms, molecules, celestial bodies and cosmic structures naturally feature the six‑layer architecture, yet all manifest as passive constraints, fixed mechanisms and no self‑regulating or self‑sustaining purposes:

1. **Component layer**: physical structures built from subordinate nodes, sub‑structures and nested topologies, naturally generating containment‑ and inheritance‑hierarchies.
2. **Architecture layer**: fixed arrangements for particle lattices, celestial orbits and network topologies, forming stable configurations.
3. **Mechanism layer**: gravitation, electromagnetism, quantum stress and phase‑synchronization constraints constitute invariant operating‑rules. These rules are not designed for self‑maintenance; they are inevitable behaviors derived from topological rules.

5. **Event layer**: phase jumps, energy‑level transitions, collision perturbations and structural disintegrations count as objective events. Events occur without serving structural continuity and merely obey global stress‑balance.
6. **Information layer**: particle phase, state, spin and energy‑levels represent primary physical information. This information objectively exists, yet it is not used for environmental‑recognition, decision‑making or self‑preservation.

#### Quasi‑systems: weak initiative without complete self‑sustaining strategies

In quasi‑systems the six‑layer structure begins to support limited adjustable mechanisms and responses:

- Preliminary environmental sensing and simple steady‑state maintenance;
- Partial capacity to counteract external perturbations;
- But no top‑level self‑preservation goals, no proactive evolution, no intentional design or strategic behavior.

#### Complete complex systems: core distinctions for higher‑order life, enterprises and societies

For genuine complex systems, attributes across all six layers are reversed:

- Architecture can be reconstructed;
- Mechanisms can adapt and self‑regulate;
- Transactions can operate strategically;
- Events may be predicted, avoided or exploited;
- Information is used for environmental perception, decision‑making and iterative evolution.

Operations across all layers serve one overarching objective: sustaining continuous existence, stability and further continuation of the whole system.
